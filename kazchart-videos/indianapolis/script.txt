In the summer of nineteen forty-five, nearly nine hundred men went into the sea. Only three hundred and sixteen came out.

This is the story of the U.S.S. Indianapolis.

A heavy cruiser with almost twelve hundred men aboard, she had just completed a top secret mission.

Delivering parts of the atomic bomb that would soon fall on Hiroshima.

Then she sailed on alone, with no escort.

Just after midnight on July thirtieth, a Japanese submarine found her.

Two torpedoes. Twelve minutes. The Indianapolis was gone.

About three hundred men went down with her.

The rest, nearly nine hundred, were left floating in the open ocean.

Few lifeboats. Little water. And no one knew they were missing.

Then the sharks came.

Oceanic whitetips, drawn by the sinking, the noise, and the blood.

Men learned to stay in tight groups. Those who drifted away were taken.

But most of the dying came from the sun, thirst, and salt water.

Some men began to hallucinate. Others drank the sea.

On the fourth day, a plane on routine patrol spotted them, by pure chance.

Of nearly twelve hundred men aboard, three hundred and sixteen survived.

It remains the worst loss at sea from a single ship in U.S. Navy history.

Her captain, Charles McVay, was court-martialed for losing his ship.

Fifty-five years later, Congress cleared his name. He did not live to see it.

In twenty seventeen, the wreck was finally found, five and a half kilometres down.

She is still there. In the dark. With the men who never left her.
