In eighteen twenty, a whale did something no one believed was possible.

It hunted a ship. And it won.

November twentieth, eighteen twenty. The middle of the Pacific Ocean.

The whaleship Essex, out of Nantucket, is fifteen months into her voyage.

Her crew of twenty is hunting the most valuable animal in the ocean.

The sperm whale.

Its giant head holds a waxy oil that lit the lamps and candles of the world.

That morning, the lookout spots a pod. The men lower their small boats and give chase.

But one whale stays behind. A huge bull.

The first mate guessed it was eighty-five feet long.

It isn't fleeing. It is lining up with the ship.

It rams the bow. The ship shudders.

Then it turns, swims off, and comes back faster.

It hits again.

The bow is smashed open. Within minutes, the Essex begins to sink.

Twenty men. Three small boats. Thousands of kilometres from the nearest continent.

The nearest islands were the Marquesas. But the officers had heard stories of cannibals there. So they chose the longest route of all. South America.

Food ran out. Then water. Men began to die.

To survive, the living did the unthinkable.

They ate the dead. In the captain's boat, they drew lots to decide who would be next.

After about ninety days, passing whaleships finally found them.

Of twenty men, only eight survived.

Years later, a young sailor named Herman Melville read the first mate's account.

In eighteen fifty-one, he turned it into a novel. You know it as Moby-Dick.

What happened to the whale, no one knows.

Was it defending its pod? Was it provoked by the hammering of the crew's repairs? Or did it know exactly what it was doing?
